# Feature: Decision Quality

A supplement to [`idea-file_arcee.md`](../idea-file_arcee.md).

**Status: optional — narrow in scope, but valuable for genuinely consequential decisions.**

---

## What This Adds

Most day-to-day decisions don't need structure — you just decide. But a decision that's consequential, hard to reverse, or genuinely difficult to reason about (a major purchase, a career move, a big family decision) benefits from something more rigorous than "talk it through in chat once and go with your gut."

This feature is a structured, multi-dimension evaluation framework, applied when a decision earns the extra weight:

- **Problem framing** — is the decision clearly scoped (timeframe, stakeholders, decision owner, reversibility)?
- **Information quality** — are the facts being used to evaluate options relevant, reliable, and complete?
- **Alternatives** — are the options under consideration substantively distinct and viable, including the option of doing nothing?
- **Value clarity and tradeoffs** — are the criteria that actually matter for this decision surfaced, weighted, and explicitly traded off against each other?
- **Risk awareness** — are downside scenarios and asymmetric outcomes honestly assessed, with real mitigations, not hand-waved?
- **Strategic/life alignment** — does this decision actually ladder up to the stated longer-term goals, or does it just feel urgent right now?
- **Execution readiness** — is there realistically the capacity, buy-in, and follow-through to implement this?

Each dimension gets scored independently — in a multi-agent implementation, by a separate reviewer per dimension, so one dimension's reasoning doesn't quietly bias another's — with rationale and, where relevant, cited sources. The result is a structured brief you build iteratively (an initial pass with a health-check summary and a pointer to the weakest, most load-bearing dimension) followed by a final synthesized analysis once the brief is complete.

## Why Structure Helps Here Specifically

Unstructured "let's think it through" conversation is genuinely good for a lot of decisions, but it has a specific failure mode on the important ones: it's easy to spend the whole conversation on the dimension that's most emotionally salient (usually risk, or values) while never actually stress-testing whether the alternatives under consideration are real alternatives, or whether the information behind the decision is actually reliable. Forcing every dimension to get evaluated — even briefly — surfaces the weak points a purely conversational pass would skip past. The framework isn't meant to replace judgment; it's meant to make sure judgment gets applied everywhere it needs to, not just where the conversation happened to linger.
